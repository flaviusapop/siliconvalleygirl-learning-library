Overview

Isomorphic Labs president Max Jaderberg explains how his team combines predictive models, generative agents and real laboratory experiments in drug discovery. He connects that research loop to commercialization, interdisciplinary hiring and the long, safety-critical path from a candidate molecule to patients.

Key points

  • Jaderberg treats AlphaFold as proof that models can generalize in biology, not as a complete drug-design system; target selection, delivery, toxicity and other challenges require additional capabilities.
  • The company develops its own fully funded, IP-owned programs alongside pharma partnerships, using real partner problems to test whether research progress matters commercially.
  • Generative models propose candidates and separate predictive models narrow the field before molecules are made and tested; Jaderberg claims some programs can test 20 or 50 rather than screening vastly larger numbers, without giving a precise hit rate.
  • A benchmark is only a proxy: when model scores improve without corresponding discovery results, Jaderberg says to redesign the evaluation around real-world work.
  • Drug-design agents must search across conflicting molecular properties rather than maximize a single score; their practical benefit needs empirical comparison with human performance and time.
  • Clinical testing and regulatory evidence remain necessary for patient safety; Jaderberg cannot predict when an AI-designed drug will reach a pharmacy or which patients will benefit first.
  • For scientists entering AI, he prioritizes disciplinary fundamentals, interdisciplinary curiosity and research taste built through hands-on experience.

Summary

Jaderberg traces Isomorphic Labs’ ambition to DeepMind’s AlphaFold results: a model could learn useful generalizations about biological structure, suggesting that other hard biological and chemical predictions might eventually become tractable. He explicitly says AlphaFold alone cannot make a medicine. A team must choose a disease mechanism and target, design a molecule that changes it, get that molecule to the right place in the body and assess safety and toxicity. His view is that a collection of further AlphaFold-like advances, not one model, must come together as a drug-design engine. He separates models for understanding disease biology and cell pathways from models for designing interventions such as small molecules, antibodies or peptides. The sweeping mission to solve disease is an organizational ambition, not evidence that those capabilities or cures already exist.

The business architecture is deliberately two-sided. Isomorphic runs its own funded programs and retains their intellectual property while also working on difficult problems brought by pharmaceutical partners. Jaderberg says outside partners prevent the company from marking its own homework and force its research to confront commercial constraints. Since traditional discovery already involves empirical trial and error, he argues that useful prediction need not be perfect: a meaningful improvement over the available baseline can guide choices. But static model benchmarks are not the finish line. If leaderboard progress does not translate into performance on live discovery programs, he says the evaluation itself must change. Internally, he breaks the long-range mission into a tree of team and individual missions with measurable milestones; one illustrative modeling target was 80% accuracy against a 10% starting level. Those numbers describe his example, not a clinical result.

The operating loop pairs creativity with selection and real-world feedback. Generative models and agents produce candidate molecular designs; separate virtual-lab models predict their properties and narrow the list; scientists then synthesize and test selected molecules. Jaderberg claims that in some work the team can make 20 or 50 candidates rather than experimentally screen hundreds of thousands, but does not disclose the hit-rate denominator, comparative study or evidence of patient benefit. Chemists increasingly define the design space and objectives instead of manually proposing every atomic change. Because desired properties can conflict, an agent must explore tradeoffs rather than greedily optimize one metric. He recommends evaluating each component against concrete outcomes, including time saved and scientific value, while acknowledging that drug-discovery results cannot simply be verified on a computer. His broader opportunity thesis is that models simulating physical systems complement reasoning models; noisy or missing training data can itself be the bottleneck.

The closing discussion puts a boundary around the optimism. Jaderberg emphasizes preclinical evidence, regulatory review and gradual clinical testing as patient-safety safeguards; he does not know when an AI-designed medicine will be available in a pharmacy, or which of his company’s programs will reach patients first. His comments about future personalized medicine and faster development are forecasts, not demonstrated clinical outcomes. For someone entering AI for science, his practical advice is to learn the underlying science as well as the tools: finite compute, laboratory capacity and human attention make choosing questions consequential. He describes research taste as a learned ability to filter questions and approaches using experience and mental frameworks, strengthened by insights borrowed across disciplines. His hiring approach similarly combines specialists who are curious enough to learn one another’s language.

Actionable learnings

  1. Map an ambitious research mission to team-level milestones, and pair each quantitative proxy with an outcome that tests whether it matters in the real workflow.
  2. For a candidate-generation system, separate proposal, predictive ranking and physical testing; track the actual experimental hit rate and costs instead of treating model scores as proof.
  3. Specify competing objectives and evaluate an agent against a human or existing workflow on quality, time and downstream value before expanding automation.
  4. Build cross-disciplinary teams and ask candidates how they approached an unfamiliar field or learned from a technical failure; reserve time for domain fundamentals and hands-on research.
