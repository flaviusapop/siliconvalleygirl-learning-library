Summary

The co-founder describes Kalshi as a venue for trading on defined future events and as a source of forecast information for people who do not trade. In her account, the subjects users request have moved from AI model capabilities toward possible effects on employment and other areas. She illustrates a market that resolves according to several specified conditions, emphasizing the work required to turn a broad question into a measurable yes-or-no proposition. That construction matters: a price answers the contract’s exact question, not every possible interpretation of the broader headline. She also discusses aggregating election-market information into an index, which compresses many underlying questions into one signal at the cost of detail.

Asked whether market odds should be trusted, she distinguishes probability from certainty: a low-probability event can still occur. She says her company has studied calibration and claims that useful calibration can appear at modest trading volumes, while also acknowledging that market type and time to resolution matter. Those assertions are not independently checked here; one outcome cannot by itself prove or disprove a probability estimate. The host and guest discuss using event contracts to offset a business promotion or possible weather-related costs, but a market contract may not match an individual’s actual loss and is not equivalent to insurance. The company’s transaction-fee business gives the guest a commercial interest in these uses; no trading recommendation follows.

On AI at work, she describes augmentation rather than saying that the company has eliminated complete roles. Her team uses AI to bring together updates and metrics for planning and to surface what has or has not been completed. She says the tools connect to company communications and documents, while acknowledging that permissions for sensitive legal and surveillance work remain difficult. She also describes unresolved problems in prioritizing product feedback and improving quality assurance, as well as a design workflow where fast experiments still receive later design attention. These are reported internal practices and aspirations, not evidence that the systems are accurate, secure or broadly transferable.

Her organizational argument runs against the idea that every founder should work alone with agents: she says dedicated engineers are needed to build reliable tools while founders attend to other responsibilities. She hopes AI will let the company expand its product range rather than simply reduce headcount. She describes direct feedback, willingness to learn and assessment of AI use as parts of hiring, and recounts a regulatory struggle over election markets as an example of pursuing available options. Finally, she encourages women founders to focus on their goals while acknowledging structural inequities and the need for investors to improve. These are her perspectives, not universally proven prescriptions.

Actionable learnings

  1. Editorial application: Read a market’s exact resolution rules and deadline before interpreting its price as an answer to a broad question.
  2. Editorial application: Assess forecast quality across many resolved events and comparable horizons instead of judging calibration from one surprising result.
  3. Editorial application: Pilot an internal AI planning workflow with explicit source permissions, human review and checks for missing or misprioritized updates.
  4. Editorial application: Before treating a contract as a hedge, compare its payout conditions with the specific loss and consider the possibility of losing the stake.
