## Summary

The guest frames disease prevention as an information and incentives problem: in her account, healthcare rewards treatment more readily than prevention, leaving individuals to seek ways to understand their own risks. She differentiates a risk associated with a single genetic variant from a polygenic score aggregating many variants. Her proposed future combines genetics with records, wearable measurements and environmental information so that a person can discuss possible interventions with the medical system or examine lifestyle choices. The host presses on how population-level genetic and environmental contributions translate to a particular individual. The guest’s examples are illustrative assertions, not a validated means to calculate an individual’s risk or decide on medication; her broad claims about testing and treatment should not be taken as clinical instructions.

She argues that larger genetic datasets and AI could improve risk prediction, and describes linking consenting participants’ wearable data and records to research. But she also identifies a crucial gap: a model finding patterns is not the same as establishing that an intervention changes outcomes. Her account emphasizes the difficulty of running experiments and waiting for clinical events, and she cautions that drug development still requires human trials and regulatory work. Her forecasts about future prediction and gene editing are speculative.
