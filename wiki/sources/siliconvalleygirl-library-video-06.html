Overview

Felix Rieseberg, Claude engineering lead at Anthropic at the time of recording, discusses designing AI workflows around outcomes, tool access, adversarial review and human feedback. He also argues that lower prototyping costs increase the importance of product judgment and understanding customers, while acknowledging that workflows and model capabilities change quickly.

Key points

  • The guest distinguishes automating unwanted work from augmenting people to do work they could not previously accomplish, and urges teams to redesign the surrounding workflow.
  • He suggests evaluating an agent’s process and evidence, not merely whether its answer happens to be correct.
  • His suggested review loop uses separate sessions to challenge an output and assess the criticisms; he prefers hard external evidence when available.
  • He recommends tool and data access with clear descriptions rather than rigidly pre-slicing context, while suggesting that old instructions or skills should be tested rather than assumed helpful.
  • To teach a review style, he proposes collecting past feedback, distilling principles and comparing model feedback with a person’s feedback side by side before delegation.
  • He argues inexpensive prototypes do not replace customer empathy, good product decisions or relationships, particularly in neglected operational software.
  • For aspiring engineers he favors demonstrable projects, customer understanding and working across systems, while warning that today’s practical advice may need revision.

Summary

The guest describes spending substantial effort on prototypes and then evaluating which ideas actually help people. As production becomes easier, he argues, the scarce resource is often attention: a team can make many variations, but users cannot meaningfully evaluate all of them. His distinction between automation and augmentation is useful for selecting opportunities. Automation removes work or reduces cost; augmentation lets someone perform a task that previously exceeded their skills or capacity. In both cases, he recommends thinking about the desired outcome and the workflow rather than adding a chatbot to an unchanged process. His claims about capabilities and development speed are practitioner observations, not independently verified performance results.

The discussion treats evaluation as a combination of process and results. In a weather example, a lucky guess should not receive the same credit as an answer obtained through an appropriate location and reputable sources, even if the latter has a small error. For writing, the guest suggests breaking expert work into concrete techniques—such as developing counterarguments—instead of merely asking a model to sound like an expert. He proposes having separate sessions attack a draft, then using another review pass to assess whether those criticisms are valid. He also says an independent external signal is preferable when obtainable. Multiple model reviews can still share blind spots and cannot substitute for checking sources or expert review on consequential questions.

On setup, the guest prefers giving a model clearly described tools through which it can retrieve relevant information, rather than automatically assigning narrow data slices and elaborate personas. He suggests periodically testing whether existing skills or instructions still improve results, because models change. His example of learning a review style starts with existing human feedback, summarizes recurring principles and compares the model’s proposed feedback with the person’s own in parallel. Only after observing the differences would a team consider changing the level of delegation. He expresses strong personal trust in an AI fact-checking workflow, but that is his judgment, not evidence of reliable fact-checking in general. Access to business data and computer controls also needs appropriate permissions and safeguards, a consideration not resolved by his account.

The product and career discussion shifts from tool mechanics to customer value. The guest predicts that cheaper building will put pressure on software people use only because they lack alternatives, but says people will still pay for products that solve real needs well. He identifies cumbersome operational software as a possible opportunity and stresses that learning the customer’s workflow and building relationships are separate from producing a prototype. For people entering software, he recommends making tangible projects, understanding users and learning to combine systems and collaborate across functions. These are hiring preferences and forecasts, not guarantees about careers or markets. He ends by emphasizing how difficult future predictions are and how quickly current advice may become outdated.

Actionable learnings

  1. Choose one workflow and specify a useful outcome, failure modes and a baseline before automating or augmenting it.
  2. Create a draft, have an independent session challenge it, and verify material criticisms against primary evidence rather than relying on model agreement.
  3. Compare a human reviewer’s feedback with AI feedback on the same sample; track disagreement before expanding delegation.
  4. Audit existing instructions and tool permissions: compare output with and without each instruction, and grant only access appropriate to the task.
  5. Interview users of a cumbersome operational workflow and test a small prototype against their actual needs.
