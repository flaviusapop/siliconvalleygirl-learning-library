## Summary

The guest describes three ways of working with AI: continuous joint problem solving, a division of work in which the person supervises delegated tasks, and handing work off with little engagement. He reports poorer learning among the last group and warns that persuasive outputs can encourage people to stop questioning. The practical implication is not that one collaboration style is always optimal: the boundary of what a model can do is uneven, and a division that worked with one model may need to be tested again. He suggests asking for counterarguments, alternative explanations and red flags, then challenging the system rather than treating a polished answer as evidence. These are his interpretations of research and practice; the underlying studies and their methods were not reviewed here.

The learning question becomes sharper when one person can use AI across several specialties. The guest argues that someone producing design or analysis still needs enough domain knowledge to judge whether the result is appropriate, not merely average. He offers a learning sequence: first work through foundations yourself, then use the machine to generate more examples and study patterns. He describes existing business-school work that combines building AI applications with discussion of strategy, economics and organizational change. He says an expanded curriculum planned for the following spring will devote half the class to building with AI. These educational approaches are not proof that any particular curriculum or exercise produces better long-term skill. The conversation acknowledges the unresolved concern that automation may remove the junior tasks through which people traditionally gain judgment.

For companies, he recommends that leaders try building or using AI themselves instead of relying entirely on secondhand reports. He says organizations should set demanding goals in terms of customer service, operations or other business results, then determine how AI and organizational changes might contribute. He also reports a gap in AI usage within organizations and notes that the usage data he discusses had not established a correlation with performance. His separate references to large productivity differences should not be read as a measured causal effect of that usage gap. A useful test would compare a defined workflow against its prior baseline and account for other influences rather than credit every improvement to the model.

The guest sees declining costs of accessing expertise as a challenge to both workers and firms: what valuable contribution remains when others can use similar models? He favors combining domain judgment, ambition and practice with the tools, while admitting uncertainty about which occupations will be disrupted and how quickly. In particular, he questions the assumption that junior roles are necessarily the most exposed and speculates about resistance in middle management; that is a forecast and interpretation, not a verified labor-market conclusion. The closing discussion also raises consumption and meaning: if production grows and agents filter material for people, both the review burden and the human experience of work may change in ways the speakers cannot yet resolve.

## Actionable learnings

1. Pick one recurring task and compare collaborative, supervised-delegation and largely automated approaches; record both output quality and what the worker learns.
2. Ask for plausible counterarguments and failure cases, then check important claims against independent evidence.
3. Practice foundational work before using AI to generate additional examples; periodically test whether you can assess the results without the model.
4. Define a business outcome and baseline before an AI pilot, and separately track adoption, quality and other causes of change.
