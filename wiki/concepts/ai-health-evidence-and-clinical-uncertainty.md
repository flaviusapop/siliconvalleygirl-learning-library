# AI in health: evidence, uncertainty and accountable care

## Distinguish a research signal from a clinical outcome

AI can appear in discussions of biomedical research, prediction, record organization and clinical decisions, but these are different activities with different evidence requirements.

The Google executive describes models generating biological hypotheses or candidate structures while laboratory synthesis and validation constrain what can be tested; these are company-associated claims, not independently confirmed clinical results.

Richard Socher imagines AI-assisted biomedical discovery but acknowledges slow physical experiments, and his disease and longevity outcomes remain speculative.

Anne Wojcicki proposes combining genetic information with records, wearable measures and environmental data to inform risk discussions, while distinguishing single-variant risks from polygenic scores; these examples do not validate an individual risk calculation or medication decision.

She also acknowledges that finding patterns is not the same as showing an intervention changes outcomes, and that drug development requires human trials and regulatory work.

**Editorial application:** When describing a proposed health tool, label whether its evidence concerns a research hypothesis, a predictive output or an actual patient outcome, and state what has not been tested; those labels do not themselves establish safety.

## Diagnosis, missing context and the limits of more testing

The UCSF physician questions comparisons that give an AI a neatly distilled case, because collecting and selecting relevant facts from an entire medical history is itself part of clinical work.

The Stanford physician describes a study in which a model alone reportedly performed differently from clinicians given access to it, but the study setting and participants’ experience limit generalization.

The Stanford physician gives examples of date confusion and copied diagnoses; the reviewed summary separately cautions that a chart summary can omit important details, and the examples and study claims were not independently checked.

The Stanford physician suggests that leading questions can influence chatbot answers, but the reviewed summary cautions that neutral wording or agreement among models is not a reliable safety protocol.

The UCSF physician describes a tension between avoiding missed diagnoses and indiscriminate testing, where incidental findings may lead to further procedures, expense or harm.

Wojcicki promotes her company’s health offering and discusses whole-body imaging, disclosing an investment in an imaging provider and acknowledging findings that can lead to further workup and anxiety; the UCSF physician questions assuming that earlier detection always improves outcomes.

Their different emphases on information and on testing harms do not settle the benefit–harm balance for any person, nor do they establish that more data is always better.

## Sensitive information, accountability and practical boundaries

The host describes assembling medical and genetic information for AI-assisted questions, while the UCSF physician notes that records can conflict; the reviewed Stanford summary separately cautions about security and retention risks.

Wojcicki describes research involving consenting participants’ records and wearable information, but her account of consent and choice is not an independent assessment of privacy safeguards.

The speakers in the discussion with Susan Wojcicki’s family discuss organizing information and preparing questions for clinician visits, including potential help from AI.

**Editorial caution:** AI can be wrong, and sharing sensitive records creates privacy risks.

**Editorial application:** Before proposing a health-data feature, document what information would be shared, who can access or retain it, and what terms apply; this checklist is not a privacy audit or a security guarantee.

**Editorial application:** Present any example question list as preparation for a qualified clinician, not as a diagnostic or triage substitute; even a carefully framed chatbot response can be wrong.

This article is not medical advice: do not use it to change medication, delay care or choose screening; discuss symptoms and testing with an appropriate clinician, and use appropriate clinical or emergency services for new, severe or urgent symptoms.
