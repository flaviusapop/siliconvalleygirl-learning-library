The business architecture is deliberately two-sided. Jaderberg says outside partners prevent the company from marking its own homework and force its research to confront commercial constraints. Since traditional discovery already involves empirical trial and error, he argues that useful prediction need not be perfect: a meaningful improvement over the available baseline can guide choices. But static model benchmarks are not the finish line. If leaderboard progress does not translate into performance on live discovery programs, he says the evaluation itself must change. Internally, he breaks the long-range mission into a tree of team and individual missions with measurable milestones; one illustrative modeling target was 80% accuracy against a 10% starting level. The operating loop pairs creativity with selection and real-world feedback. Jaderberg claims that in some work the team can make 20 or 50 candidates rather than experimentally screen hundreds of thousands, but does not disclose the hit-rate denominator, comparative study or evidence of patient benefit. Chemists increasingly define the design space and objectives instead of manually proposing every atomic change. Because desired properties can conflict, an agent must explore tradeoffs rather than greedily optimize one metric. He recommends evaluating each component against concrete outcomes, including time saved and scientific value, while acknowledging that drug-discovery results cannot simply be verified on a computer. His broader opportunity thesis is that models simulating physical systems complement reasoning models; noisy or missing training data can itself be the bottleneck.

The closing discussion puts a boundary around the optimism. For someone entering AI for science, his practical advice is to learn the underlying science as well as the tools: finite compute, laboratory capacity and human attention make choosing questions consequential. He describes research taste as a learned ability to filter questions and approaches using experience and mental frameworks, strengthened by insights borrowed across disciplines. His hiring approach similarly combines specialists who are curious enough to learn one another’s language.
